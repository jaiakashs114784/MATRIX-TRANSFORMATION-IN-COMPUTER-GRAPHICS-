02 THE HOOK

How does a screen move a picture?

  • Every picture, icon, or character you see on a screen is really just a set of points.
  • To slide, spin, or resize anything on screen, the computer simply changes those points using numbers.
  • Even this robot arm moves the same way — its elbow is just a point being recalculated every frame.
  • This set of rules for changing points is called a transformation matrix — the heart of computer graphics.
  • Today: how a small grid of numbers can move an entire picture at once.
03 THE PROBLEM

Moving pictures is trickier than it looks

  • A single image on screen can be made of thousands of points (pixels or corners of a shape).
  • Moving each point with its own separate instruction would be far too slow for smooth animation.
  • We need one simple rule that moves, turns, or resizes every point together, instantly.
  • That one rule is a matrix — a compact grid of numbers that acts like a recipe for motion.
04 FOUNDATION

Coordinate systems: X, Y, Z

  • Every point on a screen or in a 3D scene is described using coordinates.
  • Think of the screen as a giant grid: X tells you how far right, Y tells you how far up.
  • 3D graphics — games, animation, CAD — add a Z-axis for depth, going into or out of the screen.
  • Every matrix transformation works by changing these coordinate numbers for every point.
05 CORE CONCEPT

What is a matrix?

  • A matrix is simply a grid of numbers arranged in rows and columns.
  • In graphics, a matrix works like a recipe of instructions for moving points in space.
  • Multiplying a point's coordinates by the matrix gives you the point's new coordinates after the move.
  • Change a few numbers → you get a completely different motion.
  • The graphics chip in your phone or laptop performs millions of these matrix multiplications every second.
06 THE TOOLKIT

Types of transformations

  • Translation — sliding a picture from one place to another, without changing its shape.
  • Rotation — spinning a picture around a point, like turning a steering wheel.
  • Scaling — making a picture bigger or smaller, like zooming in or out.
  • Every complex animation in a game or movie is built from just these three basic moves, combined.
07 TRANSFORMATION 01

Translation — sliding a picture

  • Translation slides every point of a picture by the same amount, without turning or resizing it.
  • Only the last column of the matrix changes — it stores "how far" to move in X, Y (and Z in 3D).
  • Graphics use-case: sliding a game character across the screen, or moving a window from one side to another.
  • Think: "shift everything 5 steps right, 3 steps up" — that shift is stored as numbers in the matrix.
08 TRANSFORMATION 02

Rotation — turning a picture

  • Rotation spins a picture around a fixed point, called the pivot, by some angle θ.
  • The matrix uses the sine and cosine of that angle to work out each point's new position.
  • Graphics use-case: a spinning loading icon, a rotating steering wheel in a racing game, or a turning logo.
  • Rotating by 90°, 180°, or any custom angle just means plugging a different θ into the same formula.
09 TRANSFORMATION 03

Scaling — resizing a picture

  • Scaling multiplies every point's coordinates by a factor: 2× doubles the size, 0.5× halves it.
  • You use scaling every time you zoom into a photo, resize a window, or make a game sprite bigger as it gets closer.
  • Scale factors sit on the diagonal of the matrix — one number for each axis (X, Y, and Z).
  • Scaling can be uneven too: stretching an image wider without making it taller uses different X and Y factors.
10 THE SUPERPOWER

Combining transformations

  • Want to move a picture, then rotate it, then resize it? Just multiply the matrices together.
  • The result is one combined matrix that does all three transformations in a single step.
  • Order matters: rotate → move ≠ move → rotate — try it and you'll land in a different spot.
  • This is why matrices beat long lists of instructions — one precomputed, compact result applied instantly to every point.
11 CASE STUDY

Bonus: matrices beyond the screen

  • Matrix transformations aren't only for pictures — the exact same math controls robot arms in factories.
  • A camera spots a part on a conveyor belt and turns its position into coordinates, just like a point in a graphics scene.
  • The controller multiplies a chain of matrices — one per joint — to find exactly where the gripper must go.
  • It's the same idea as animating a character on screen: chain simple transformations to get one precise, final result.
12 WALKTHROUGH

Step-by-step: drawing one frame

  • Step 1 — Start with the object's own shape, described in its own local coordinates.
  • Step 2 — Apply a matrix to move, rotate, and scale it into place in the scene — its world position.
  • Step 3 — Apply another matrix to convert that into what the camera/viewer actually sees.
  • Step 4 — The final coordinates are drawn as pixels — this repeats dozens of times per second for smooth motion.
13 WHY IT MATTERS

Why matrices are important

  • One tool for everything — position, rotation, and size all use the same kind of math.
  • Fast — a single matrix multiply can move thousands of points at once, keeping games smooth.
  • Reliable — the same numbers always give the exact same result, no guessing involved.
  • Scalable — the same idea works for a simple 2D icon or a fully 3D animated character.
  • That's why matrices are taught early in graphics, animation, and game design courses.
14 BEYOND THE BASICS

Applications in computer graphics

  • Video games — moving characters, rotating cameras, and scaling objects in real time.
  • Animated movies & VFX — every character motion is built from chained transformation matrices.
  • Phone & app interfaces — sliding menus, zooming photos, rotating icons.
  • Virtual & augmented reality — tracking head movement and redrawing the 3D world instantly.
  • CAD & 3D modelling — rotating and resizing designs as you work.
  • Robotics — the same matrix math also drives robot arms and drones.
15 MISSION COMPLETE

Conclusion: matrices = motion on screen

  • Every image that moves, spins, or resizes on a screen is matrix math running behind the scenes.
  • Translation slides, rotation turns, scaling resizes — three simple ideas that build all visual motion.
  • Multiply the matrices together, and complicated animations become one small, fast calculation.
  • Learn transformations well, and you understand the core language of games, movies, and virtual worlds.
